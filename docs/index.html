⚠ Analyst Note — Decile 10 Tail Over-Prediction

Decile 10 A/E = 0.74: the model predicts $48,931 on average but actual mean claims are $36,399 — an over-prediction of ~$12,500 per policy. This is a well-documented behaviour of Gamma GLMs with a log link.

Why it happens: The log link is multiplicative. High-risk factor combinations (older age + high BMI + tobacco use) compound exponentially in the extreme tail where training data is sparse, causing the model to overshoot.

Production mitigations:

  • Winsorise large claims — cap the top 1–5% of charges before fitting so the GLM does not over-extrapolate to extreme observations.
  • Two-component large-loss model — fit the main Gamma GLM below a threshold (e.g. $25k) and a separate Pareto/lognormal for the tail, blended with a mixing weight.
  • Tweedie GLM — a compound Poisson-Gamma model that adapts tail behaviour via an estimated power parameter.
  • A/E monitoring — in a live portfolio this segment would be flagged for a rate revision at the next annual filing.

Why a Gamma GLM with a log link

Healthcare costs are strictly positive and right-skewed: most members cost little, a small number cost a lot. A Gamma distribution fits that shape far better than a normal distribution would, and the log link makes every rating factor act multiplicatively, which is exactly how a real rate manual is built: a base rate multiplied by a relativity for each factor.

Frequency and severity are combined here

This dataset only has one total annual cost per person, not individual claim records, so this model is effectively a single severity-style cost model rather than a true frequency × severity split. A production pricing team with claims-level data would model utilization (visits or admissions per member) and cost-per-service separately, then multiply them, because the same total cost can come from many small claims or a few large ones, and that distinction matters for deductible design and reinsurance.

Regulatory constraints on rating factors

Not every statistically significant factor is legally usable. Under the EU Gender Directive, insurers across the EU cannot price by sex at all. Under the US Affordable Care Act, individual and small-group health plans can only vary premium by age (capped at a 3:1 ratio), tobacco use (capped at 1.5:1), geography, and family size, health status and sex are excluded by law.

That restriction lines up with this model's own results: sex came out statistically insignificant here (p = 0.128) even before any regulation is applied, while age, BMI, dependents, tobacco use, and two of the three regional bands were all significant at p < 0.05.

Limitations of this build

The data is a single cross-sectional snapshot of 1,338 people, not multi-year claims experience, so there is no credibility weighting for thin segments and no out-of-sample holdout test, the fit statistics above are in-sample. A production model would validate on a held-out year, monitor actual-to-expected loss ratios by segment over time, and blend small or new segments toward the class average using credibility theory rather than trusting the raw GLM output.
